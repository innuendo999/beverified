Identity theft at scale rarely starts with a forged passport. It starts with a database: credit files, scraped profiles, an exposed customer table, a bribed support agent. The stolen details then feed account takeovers, loan fraud, SIM swaps, phishing and synthetic identities for years afterwards. We picked the cases below because they were large, because they changed how regulators or courts treat the companies involved, and because each one has a public record we could check. Every figure comes from a regulator, a court filing, a company statement or a major news report linked in the text.

## Equifax, 2017: the credit bureau breach

The settlement announced in July 2019 by the [CFPB, the FTC and the states](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-ftc-states-announce-settlement-with-equifax-over-2017-data-breach) covered about 147 million US consumers whose names, addresses, Social Security numbers and dates of birth were exposed. Equifax agreed to pay up to $700 million, including up to $425 million for consumer relief and a $100 million civil penalty to the CFPB.

In February 2020 a federal grand jury in Atlanta returned a nine-count indictment against four members of China's People's Liberation Army. The [Justice Department said](https://www.justice.gov/opa/speech/attorney-general-william-p-barr-announces-indictment-four-members-china-s-military) the hackers got in through a vulnerability in Equifax's online dispute resolution portal and took personal data on nearly 150 million Americans, plus driver's licence numbers for at least 10 million.

What it means for verification: once a full set of identifiers for half a country's adults is in criminal hands, knowledge-based checks ("confirm your SSN and date of birth") stop proving much. Anyone can answer them.

## Facebook, 2021: 533 million scraped profiles

In April 2021 a dataset covering more than 530 million Facebook users circulated online. [Meta said](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/04/facts-on-news-reports-about-facebook-data/) the data was scraped before September 2019 by abusing its contact importer feature, and did not include financial information, health data or passwords. What it did include, phone numbers tied to names, is exactly what SIM-swap and phishing crews need.

Ireland's [Data Protection Commission fined Meta €265 million](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/news-media/press-releases/data-protection-commission-announces-decision-in-facebook-data-scraping-inquiry) in November 2022. The decision found breaches of GDPR Article 25 (data protection by design and by default) for the period from May 2018 to September 2019, and covered the Facebook Search, Messenger Contact Importer and Instagram Contact Importer tools.

## LinkedIn, 2021: 700 million records that were "not a breach"

Two months later a seller claimed to have data on 700 million LinkedIn users. The records included names, email addresses, phone numbers, physical addresses, geolocation and professional background, according to [Malwarebytes' report](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2021/06/second-colossal-linkedin-breach-in-3-months-almost-all-users-affected). LinkedIn's response: "This was not a LinkedIn data breach and our investigation has determined that no private LinkedIn member data was exposed."

The company is technically right, and that is the lesson. Aggregated public data is enough to build a convincing profile of a real person, or a plausible fake one. A KYC flow that checks whether a name, employer and email "look consistent" will pass such profiles.

## Optus and Medibank, 2022: Australia's double hit

Two Australian breaches in late 2022 exposed government ID numbers and health records for a large share of the population.

At Optus, the regulator alleges that about 9.5 million current, former and prospective customers were affected. The data included names, dates of birth, addresses and contact details, plus passport numbers, driver's licence numbers, Medicare card numbers and birth and marriage certificate information. Some of it was later released on the dark web. In August 2025 the [Australian Information Commissioner filed civil penalty proceedings](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/oaic-takes-civil-penalty-action-against-optus) against Optus in the Federal Court, alleging one contravention of the Privacy Act for each affected person. The maximum penalty is A$2.22 million per contravention; the court will decide whether any penalty applies and how much. Our 2025 article cited "up to 10 million" affected, based on early reporting; the regulator's figure is the one we now use.

Medibank, a health insurer, notified the regulator of its own breach on 25 October 2022. The [OAIC began proceedings in June 2024](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/oaic-takes-civil-penalty-action-against-medibank), alleging that Medibank failed to take reasonable steps to protect the personal and sensitive health information of 9.7 million Australians. That data was also published on the dark web.

For identity teams the Optus case matters more than its size suggests: passport and licence numbers are the details used in database-backed ID checks. When those numbers leak, a "document number matches the government record" result no longer proves the applicant holds the document. Reading the chip in an [NFC passport](/publications/nfc-enabled-passports-what-compliance-teams-need-to-know/) or authenticating the physical document is a stronger test.

## National Public Data, 2024: a data broker goes bankrupt

National Public Data sold background-check data. In 2024 a hacker using the name USDoD claimed 2.9 billion records from it, and researcher Troy Hunt estimated around 899 million unique Social Security numbers in the set, though it contains many duplicates. The company's own breach notice in Maine reported 1.3 million people affected. The exposed fields were names, Social Security numbers, phone numbers, addresses and dates of birth.

Its parent, Jerico Pictures, filed for Chapter 11 in the US Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Florida on 2 October 2024, telling the court that "this data breach caused a massive exposure of this data", [according to The Record](https://therecord.media/national-public-data-bankruptcy-cyberattack). Several class actions had been filed against it.

The gap between "1.3 million" and "2.9 billion" is worth noticing. Breach headlines often repeat the attacker's number. The official notice can be very different, and the honest answer is often that no one knows the true count.

## Coinbase, 2025: insiders sell customer data

In May 2025 Coinbase disclosed that criminals had paid overseas customer support contractors to pass them customer data. The company refused a $20 million extortion demand. Its Maine notification listed 69,461 affected users, and the exposed data included images of passports and driving licences, [The Register reported](https://www.theregister.com/2025/05/21/coinbase_confirms_insider_breach_affects/). Coinbase's SEC filing estimated the cost at $180 million to $400 million. In December 2025, [Hyderabad police arrested a former customer service agent](https://www.theweek.in/news/biz-tech/2025/12/27/crypto-coinbase-ceo-hyderabad-police-arrest-ex-customer-service-agent.amp.html).

This one is uncomfortable for our industry. The ID images were collected for KYC, and the leak came from people with legitimate access. Data minimisation, access logging and retention limits on KYC images are part of fraud prevention, not just privacy compliance.

## The North Korean laptop farms, 2024 to 2025

This is the most literal identity theft case on the list. North Korean IT workers used stolen US identities to get remote jobs, with US-based facilitators hosting company laptops at home so the workers appeared to be in the country.

In July 2025 Christina Chapman of Arizona was sentenced in federal court in Washington, DC to 102 months in prison. The [Justice Department said](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/arizona-woman-sentenced-17m-information-technology-worker-fraud-scheme-generated-revenue) her scheme used 68 identities stolen from US victims, defrauded 309 US businesses and generated more than $17 million in illicit revenue, much of it reported to the IRS and the Social Security Administration under the stolen names.

Earlier, on 30 June 2025, the department announced [coordinated actions in 16 states](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-coordinated-nationwide-actions-combat-north-korean-remote) against similar schemes, including searches of 29 known or suspected laptop farms. One Massachusetts case alone involved more than 80 compromised US identities and jobs at more than 100 US companies. Those allegations remain unproven until tried.

These schemes beat HR onboarding, not bank KYC, but the failure mode is the same: a real person's identifiers paired with someone else's face. A selfie match against a document, with [liveness detection](/providers/liveness-detection/), is the control designed for that gap.

## What compliance teams should take from these cases

Across the cases, the pattern is the same. Identifiers leak in bulk, they get reused for years, and the controls that rely on "the details match" weaken each time.

- Treat static personal data as public. Name, date of birth, address, SSN or ID number can be bought. Matching them is a starting point, not proof.
- Authenticate the document and the person. Document forensics or chip reads, plus face match and liveness, are covered in our [identity verification rankings](/providers/identity-verification/).
- Add signals the fraudster cannot copy from a breach. Device, IP, email and phone history catch reused or synthetic identities before the document step. [SEON](/providers/seon/) is one provider built around that kind of digital footprint data, and our [OSINT for fraud prevention](/publications/how-to-use-osint-for-fraud-prevention/) guide covers the manual side.
- Protect what you collect. Coinbase shows that a KYC archive is itself a target.
- Keep screening after onboarding. Accounts opened with stolen identities often show up later through transaction patterns, which is where [AML monitoring tools](/providers/aml/) come in.
