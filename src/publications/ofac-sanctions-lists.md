When people say "the OFAC list" they usually mean one list, the SDN list. In reality OFAC runs dozens of sanctions programmes and publishes several lists, each with its own consequences. A bank's screening program has to know all of them, and it has to keep up, because the programmes change constantly. Syria is the clearest example: when we first published this guide in April 2025, Syria was under comprehensive US sanctions. By October 2026 that programme is gone.

## What OFAC is

The Office of Foreign Assets Control is the US Treasury's sanctions office. In [its own words](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1), it administers and enforces economic and trade sanctions against foreign jurisdictions, regimes and actors that threaten US national security, foreign policy or the economy. Targets include terrorists, narcotics traffickers and weapons proliferators. Sanctions can block the property of specific people and companies, or restrict dealings with a whole country or region through an embargo.

[OFAC FAQ 11](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/11) sets out who has to comply: all US persons, meaning US citizens and permanent residents wherever they are, anyone in the United States, US-incorporated entities and their foreign branches, and, under some programmes, foreign subsidiaries owned or controlled by US persons. Non-US persons can also break US sanctions by causing a US person to violate them or by evading them.

## The SDN list and the non-SDN lists

The **Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons (SDN) list** is the core. If a party is on it, US persons must block its property and generally cannot deal with it without OFAC authorisation.

OFAC's [other sanctions lists page](https://ofac.treasury.gov/other-ofac-sanctions-lists) shows six non-SDN lists as of October 2026:

| List | What it covers | Last updated |
|---|---|---|
| SSI | Persons in sectors of the Russian economy; restricts specific debt, equity and energy project dealings | 14 September 2026 |
| FSE | Foreign sanctions evaders under E.O. 13608 | 18 December 2025 (now empty) |
| NS-MBS | Persons under "less than full blocking" menu-based sanctions | 17 July 2026 |
| NS-PLC | Palestinian Legislative Council members not on the SDN list | 27 July 2026 |
| CAPTA | Foreign banks subject to correspondent or payable-through account sanctions | 6 April 2022 |
| NS-CMIC | Chinese military-industrial complex companies | 16 December 2021 |

The FSE list is a 2025 change worth noting: [OFAC FAQ 336](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/336) says OFAC removed its last name on 18 December 2025, but names can be added at any time. Our explainer on [SDN vs SSI vs FSE lists](/publications/sdn-vs-ssi-vs-fse-lists/) covers the differences in detail.

## Active sanctions programmes in 2026

OFAC's [programmes page](https://ofac.treasury.gov/sanctions-programs-and-country-information) lists 36 active programmes in October 2026. They fall into two broad groups.

**Country and regional programmes** include Belarus, Burma, Cuba, Iran, Iraq, Lebanon, Libya, Mali, Nicaragua, North Korea, Russian Harmful Foreign Activities, Ukraine-/Russia-related, Sudan and Darfur, Venezuela and Yemen, among others. Some are close to full embargoes, others are lists of named targets. The page does not label them, so check each programme's own page before assuming what is prohibited.

**Thematic programmes** cover counter terrorism, counter narcotics, transnational criminal organisations, cyber activity, Global Magnitsky (human rights and corruption), non-proliferation, the International Criminal Court, and hostage-taking.

Russia remains the busiest area. The Russian Harmful Foreign Activities programme was last updated on 15 October 2026. Since 2022 the larger Russia risk has moved onto the SDN list: on 21 November 2024, for example, Treasury [designated Gazprombank](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USTREAS/bulletins/3c32d96) and six of its foreign subsidiaries, along with more than 50 other Russian banks.

## What changed for Syria

The old version of this guide listed Syria as a country under OFAC sanctions. That is no longer accurate.

According to [OFAC FAQ 1220](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1220):

- [Executive Order 14312](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/06/providing-for-the-revocation-of-syria-sanctions/) removed sanctions on Syria from 1 July 2025 and revoked six Syria executive orders. People blocked only under those orders came off the SDN list.
- OFAC removed the Syrian Sanctions Regulations from the Code of Federal Regulations on 26 August 2025.
- Congress repealed the Caesar Act on 18 December 2025.
- Syria's State Sponsor of Terrorism designation was rescinded on 24 August 2026, and Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham was removed as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist the same day.

Sanctions on Bashar al-Assad and other targets continue; the same order rewrote E.O. 13894 to cover former regime officials and Captagon trafficking. OFAC renamed its remaining Syria-related rules the [Promoting Accountability for Assad and Regional Stabilization Sanctions Regulations](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-09-25/html/2025-18618.htm) in September 2025, and PAARSS now appears in the programme list. Violations before 1 July 2025 can still be enforced. Our [Syria news item](/news/ofac-removes-syria-sanctions-regulations/) has the full timeline.

## The 50 Percent Rule

Screening names is not enough. Under OFAC's 50 Percent Rule, any entity owned 50 percent or more by one or more blocked persons is blocked too, whether or not it is listed. Stakes are added up across blocked owners and through intermediate companies. [OFAC FAQ 401](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/401) gives the examples: if blocked person X owns 50 percent of company A and A owns 50 percent of company B, B is blocked. If X owns 25 percent of A and 25 percent of B, and they each own 50 percent of C, C is not blocked, because neither A nor B is.

This is why banks pair sanctions screening with beneficial ownership checks. Our [KYB rankings](/providers/kyb/) compare tools that build ownership trees from registry data.

## What banks are expected to do

There is no single OFAC rule that prescribes a screening program. The FFIEC's [BSA/AML examination manual](https://bsaaml.ffiec.gov/manual/OfficeOfForeignAssetsControl/01) says one is "not required by specific regulation" but expects a risk-based program anyway, with these elements:

- Screen new accounts before opening or shortly after, and block anything beyond the initial deposit until screening is done.
- Screen funds transfers, letters of credit and non-customer transactions before they are executed.
- Update lists promptly and check existing customers when the lists change.
- Test the program independently, name a responsible person, and train staff.

Two record and reporting rules matter. Blocked property and rejected transactions have to be reported to OFAC within 10 business days. And since 21 March 2025, records must be kept for at least 10 years under [31 CFR 501.601](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-501/subpart-C/section-501.601), up from five. The change followed April 2024 legislation that doubled the statute of limitations for IEEPA and TWEA violations to 10 years ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2025/4/us-treasury-extends-recordkeeping-requirement-for-economic-sanctions-compliance-to-10-years)).

## Penalties and enforcement

OFAC's yearly totals swing widely, because one large case can dominate a year:

| Year | Penalties and settlements | Total |
|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 17 | $1,541,380,594 |
| 2024 | 12 | $48,790,404 |
| 2025 | 14 | $265,746,819 |
| 2026 (to 13 October) | 9 | $295,983,600 |

Sources: OFAC enforcement pages for [2023](https://ofac.treasury.gov/civil-penalties-and-enforcement-information/2023-enforcement-information), [2024](https://ofac.treasury.gov/civil-penalties-and-enforcement-information/2024-enforcement-information), [2025](https://ofac.treasury.gov/civil-penalties-and-enforcement-information/2025-enforcement-information) and [2026](https://ofac.treasury.gov/civil-penalties-and-enforcement-information). Binance's $968.6 million settlement made up most of 2023. In 2026, a $275 million settlement with Adani Enterprises accounts for most of the total.

The most useful 2026 case for banks is smaller. On 13 October 2026 Bank of America settled 475 apparent violations for $11,662,930. OFAC's [enforcement release](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/937231/download) points to delayed watchlist updates, an alert suppression rule, analysts clearing true matches, and a ranking flaw that treated exact name and date of birth matches as low or medium priority. OFAC also stressed that relying on a screening vendor does not transfer responsibility.
