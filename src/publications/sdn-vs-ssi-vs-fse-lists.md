OFAC, the US Treasury's sanctions office, publishes more than one list, and they do not carry the same consequences. A hit on the SDN list means you block and report. A hit on the SSI list means certain types of deals are off limits while others are allowed. Mixing them up leads either to frozen payments that should have gone through or to prohibited deals that should have been stopped. This guide explains the three lists people most often compare, plus the other non-SDN lists OFAC still maintains, as of October 2026.

## The short answer

| | SDN list | SSI list | FSE list |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full name | Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons | Sectoral Sanctions Identifications | Foreign Sanctions Evaders |
| Who is on it | Individuals, companies and other parties designated under many programmes | Companies in specified sectors of the Russian economy | Foreign persons who violated or helped evade US sanctions on Iran |
| Legal basis | Many executive orders and statutes | E.O. 13662 and Directives 1 to 4 | E.O. 13608 |
| Effect | Property blocked; US persons generally may not deal with them | Only the dealings described in the relevant directive are prohibited | Transactions by US persons or within the US involving FSEs are prohibited |
| 50 Percent Rule | Yes | Yes, for the directive prohibitions | Not addressed on OFAC's FSE page |
| Status in October 2026 | Active | Active, last updated 14 September 2026 | Empty since 18 December 2025 |

The SSI and FSE lists are "non-SDN" lists. [OFAC says](https://ofac.treasury.gov/other-ofac-sanctions-lists) that neither is part of the SDN list, but that persons on them may also appear on the SDN list.

## The SDN list

The SDN list is the one most people mean when they say "the OFAC list". It holds parties designated under programmes covering countries, terrorism, narcotics, cyber activity, human rights abuses and more. When a party is on the SDN list, US persons must block its property and interests in property that come into their possession, and they generally cannot deal with it unless OFAC authorises the dealing.

The SDN list also reaches companies that are not named on it. Under OFAC's 50 Percent Rule, an entity owned 50 percent or more, directly or indirectly, by one or more blocked persons is itself blocked. [OFAC FAQ 401](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/401) works through examples: if blocked person X owns 50 percent of company A, and A owns 50 percent of company B, then B is blocked too. That is why sanctions screening needs ownership data, not just a name match. Our [KYB provider rankings](/providers/kyb/) cover tools that map beneficial ownership.

For a fuller tour of the SDN list and OFAC programmes, see our guide to [OFAC sanctions lists](/publications/ofac-sanctions-lists/).

## The SSI list

OFAC publishes the [SSI list](https://ofac.treasury.gov/consolidated-sanctions-list/sectoral-sanctions-identifications-ssi-list) to identify persons operating in sectors of the Russian economy identified under Executive Order 13662; the first directives date from July 2014. Unlike the SDN list, it does not freeze anyone's assets. Each entry is tied to a directive, and the directive sets out what is prohibited.

According to [OFAC FAQ 371](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/371):

- **Directive 1** prohibits dealing in new debt longer than a set tenor and new equity of the listed entities. The tenor for Directive 1 has been 14 days for debt issued on or after 28 November 2017.
- **Directive 2** covers new debt longer than 60 days for debt issued on or after 28 November 2017, but not equity.
- **Directive 3** covers new debt longer than 30 days.
- **Directive 4** restricts support for certain deepwater, Arctic offshore and shale oil projects.

Older debt, shorter-tenor debt and, for Directive 2 and 3 entities, new equity remain permitted under these directives, unless another sanctions authority prohibits them. The 50 Percent Rule applies here as well: [OFAC FAQ 373](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/373) confirms the directive prohibitions extend to entities owned 50 percent or more by persons subject to the directives.

Since 2022, the bigger Russia-related risk often sits on the SDN list instead. On 21 November 2024, for example, Treasury [designated Gazprombank](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USTREAS/bulletins/3c32d96) and six of its foreign subsidiaries under E.O. 14024, along with more than 50 other Russian banks. A company can carry SSI tags and an SDN entry at once, and the SDN entry wins: full blocking applies.

## The FSE list

The [FSE list](https://ofac.treasury.gov/consolidated-sanctions-list/foreign-sanctions-evaders-fse-list) is based on Executive Order 13608. It names foreign individuals and entities that violated, attempted or conspired to violate, or caused a violation of US sanctions on Iran, and foreign persons who facilitated deceptive transactions for sanctioned persons. Transactions by US persons or within the United States involving FSEs are prohibited. Older articles (including the first version of this one) also mention Syria, because E.O. 13608 ([signed on 1 May 2012](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/DCPD-201200328/html/DCPD-201200328.htm)) targets foreign sanctions evaders "with respect to Iran and Syria"; Syria-related sanctions have since been largely removed, as our [news item on the Syria rule](/news/ofac-removes-syria-sanctions-regulations/) explains.

The big change since 2025: the list is empty. [OFAC FAQ 336](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/336) says that on 18 December 2025 OFAC removed the last remaining name from the FSE list. OFAC also says names may be added at any time, so screening systems should keep the FSE list as a source even while it returns no hits.

## Other OFAC non-SDN lists

OFAC's [other sanctions lists page](https://ofac.treasury.gov/other-ofac-sanctions-lists) currently shows six non-SDN lists:

- **SSI** (see above), last updated 14 September 2026.
- **FSE** (see above), empty since 18 December 2025.
- **NS-MBS** (Non-SDN Menu-Based Sanctions), for persons under sanctions that are "less than full blocking sanctions". If full blocking applies, the person goes on the SDN list instead. Last updated 17 July 2026.
- **NS-PLC** (Non-SDN Palestinian Legislative Council), linked to a general licence that lets US financial institutions reject transactions with PLC members not named on the SDN list. Last updated 27 July 2026.
- **CAPTA**, foreign financial institutions subject to correspondent or payable-through account sanctions. It excludes institutions already on the SDN list. Last updated 6 April 2022.
- **NS-CMIC** (Non-SDN Chinese Military-Industrial Complex Companies), last updated 16 December 2021.

The first version of this article also listed NS-ISA as a separate list. It does not appear on OFAC's list page today, so we have dropped it.

## How to screen all of them

OFAC's free [Sanctions List Search](https://sanctionssearch.ofac.treas.gov/) tool searches the SDN list together with the FSE, SSI, CAPTA and NS-PLC lists, and it tells users to check the programme code on each result, because that code says how a true hit should be treated. OFAC also warns that the tool does not replace proper due diligence.

For a business screening thousands of customers or payments, manual searches do not scale. OFAC's [Framework for OFAC Compliance Commitments](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/16331/download) lists sanctions screening software faults as a root cause of past violations, specifically failing to update screening software for changes to the SDN and SSI lists, missing identifiers such as SWIFT BICs, and missing alternative spellings.

What to check in a screening tool:

- It ingests every OFAC list, not just the SDN list, and shows which list and programme each hit came from.
- It applies the 50 Percent Rule using ownership data.
- It updates quickly after OFAC publishes changes, and re-screens existing customers against the change.
- It keeps an audit trail of each alert decision.
