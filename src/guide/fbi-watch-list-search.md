People search "FBI watch list search" for two different reasons. Some want to know whether they, or someone they are about to hire or do business with, are on a government terrorist list. Others work in compliance and assume their bank or fintech should be checking customers against it. The answer is the same for both: you cannot search it. What you can search, and what regulators actually expect businesses to screen, are other lists. This guide explains which ones, updated for the changes since our first version in May 2025.

## What the FBI watch list actually is

The "FBI watch list" is the US government's consolidated terrorist watchlist. It is run by a center within the FBI's National Security Branch. Until 2025 that center was called the Terrorist Screening Center; it is now the **Threat Screening Center**.

According to the [FBI's own page](https://www.fbi.gov/about/leadership-and-structure/national-security-branch/tsc), the center was set up after 9/11 to combine several earlier terrorism watchlists into one federal list of people reasonably suspected of involvement in terrorism or related activities. Key points from that page:

- Only government agencies can nominate someone, and specific intelligence-related criteria must be met.
- Nominations cannot be based on race, ethnicity, religion, First Amendment-protected activity, or hunches.
- Most people on the list are not US citizens and have no known US connection.
- A small subset of listed people are on the No Fly list.
- Users include the State Department, CBP, TSA, USCIS and the Department of Defense, and some data is exported to the FBI's National Crime Information Center.

### Why the name changed

The FBI renamed the center in 2025 because its mission now goes beyond terrorism. [Homeland Security Today](https://www.hstoday.us/subject-matter-areas/counterterrorism/the-terrorist-screening-center-changes-name-to-the-threat-screening-center-fbi-says/) reported that the center is expanding its watchlisting of cartel and gang members recently designated as foreign terrorist organisations, and covers transnational organised crime more broadly. [Hoodline's March 2025 report](https://hoodline.com/2025/03/fbi-s-terrorist-screening-center-rebrands-as-threat-screening-center-to-combat-evolving-national-dangers-including-drug-cartels/) quoted the center's director, Michael Glasheen: "With expanding and growing threats, we are reflecting that in our name."

## Can you search the FBI watch list?

No. The list is not published, and the FBI says the center **does not confirm anyone's status on the watchlist** for security reasons. Any website offering an "FBI watch list lookup" is searching something else: usually public sanctions lists, wanted-person pages or a commercial database.

The FBI also clears up two common misunderstandings:

- Extra screening at the airport does not mean you are on the watchlist.
- Being told you are on a "watchlist" when you are refused a loan or credit does not mean you are on the terrorist watchlist. The FBI says the list is **never used for financial decisions** such as loans, money transfers or unemployment benefits.

That second point matters for compliance teams. When a bank says a payment hit a watchlist, it almost always means a sanctions list or the bank's own internal list, not the FBI's.

## What you can search instead

### FBI Most Wanted

The FBI's public [Most Wanted pages](https://www.fbi.gov/wanted) list the Ten Most Wanted Fugitives, Most Wanted Terrorists, fugitives, fraudsters, kidnappings and missing persons, and "Seeking Information" appeals. These are public appeals for help, not a screening list, but they are what the public can actually see.

### OFAC sanctions lists

For businesses, the relevant terrorism list is OFAC's SDN list. It includes people and groups designated as Specially Designated Global Terrorists and parties linked to designated foreign terrorist organisations. US persons must block their property and cannot deal with them.

The overlap with the FBI's widened mission is direct. On 20 February 2025 the State Department designated eight cartels and gangs, including Tren de Aragua, MS-13, the Sinaloa Cartel and CJNG, under the terrorism authorities, and OFAC added them to the SDN list ([Holland & Knight summary](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/02/trump-administration-announces-designation-of-international-cartels)). A bank that screens against the SDN list already screens for those groups.

Anyone can check names for free with OFAC's [Sanctions List Search](https://sanctionssearch.ofac.treas.gov/), which covers the SDN list and OFAC's non-SDN lists. OFAC notes that the tool does not replace due diligence. Our guide to [OFAC sanctions lists](/publications/ofac-sanctions-lists/) explains what each list means.

### FinCEN 314(a) requests

There is one channel where law enforcement names reach banks directly. Under section 314(a) of the USA PATRIOT Act, federal, state, local and certain foreign law enforcement agencies can ask FinCEN to send requests to financial institutions. According to FinCEN's [314(a) fact sheet](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/shared/314afactsheet.pdf):

- FinCEN notifies institutions every two weeks that new requests are posted on a secure portal.
- Institutions have two weeks to search for accounts held by the subject in the past 12 months and transactions in the past six months.
- They report positive matches only.
- The information is a lead, not a substitute for a subpoena.

As of the fact sheet's 13 October 2026 update, FinCEN had processed 9,529 requests: 960 related to terrorism or terrorist financing and 8,569 to money laundering. A 314(a) request is a one-off search of existing records, not a watchlist you screen new customers against.

## What travellers can do: DHS TRIP

If you think you have been wrongly flagged while travelling, the route is the [DHS Traveler Redress Inquiry Program](https://www.dhs.gov/dhs-trip). It is meant for people who were denied or delayed boarding, delayed at a border crossing, or repeatedly sent to secondary screening. You fill in an online form, DHS routes it to the agencies involved, and you receive a final determination letter. You also get a redress control number, which you can add to airline bookings. The FBI notes that most people who use DHS TRIP are not on the watchlist at all.

## Watchlist screening for financial institutions

Since banks cannot see the FBI list, their watchlist screening is built around sanctions, PEP and adverse media data. The FFIEC's [BSA/AML manual](https://bsaaml.ffiec.gov/manual/OfficeOfForeignAssetsControl/01) expects US banks to:

- screen new accounts before opening or shortly after;
- screen funds transfers and other transactions before they are executed;
- update lists promptly and check existing customers when OFAC's lists change.

Getting this wrong is expensive even for banks with mature systems. In October 2026 OFAC settled with Bank of America for $11,662,930 over 475 apparent violations, including terrorism and narcotics programme breaches. OFAC's [enforcement release](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/937231/download) cited delayed watchlist updates and a ranking flaw that treated exact name and date of birth matches to blocked persons as low or medium priority.

A practical checklist for a screening setup:

1. Screen against all OFAC lists, plus UN, UK and EU lists where you operate.
2. Screen at onboarding, on each payment and whenever the lists change.
3. Use full identifiers (date of birth, nationality, ID numbers) to cut false positives.
4. Have a written process for true hits, possible hits and obvious false matches.
5. Keep a record of each decision; OFAC records must now be kept for 10 years.
