Sanctions lists and PEP databases only tell you about people someone has already put on a list. A customer under investigation for fraud, or a company director named in a bribery case, can stay off every list for years. Negative news screening is how compliance teams find that risk earlier.

## What adverse media screening covers

The [Wolfsberg Group](https://wolfsberg-group.org/resources/168/118), an association of global banks that writes financial crime guidance, defines negative news as "information available in the public domain" that a bank would consider relevant to managing financial crime risk. In practice that means news outlets, court and enforcement reports, and other open sources that mention a customer, beneficial owner or director in connection with offences such as fraud, corruption, money laundering or terrorist financing.

Wolfsberg treats it as a supplement to customer due diligence, separate from list-based sanctions and PEP checks. It applies to prospective customers at onboarding and to existing customers over the relationship. For business customers the people behind the company matter as much as the entity, which is why adverse media is a standard part of [KYB checks](/providers/kyb/).

## Why manual searching breaks down

Typing a name into a search engine does not scale, and Wolfsberg spells out why it is unreliable. Results change with the user and the date, content gets removed, and search engines are not built for financial crime risk. Three other problems come up constantly:

- Volume: Common names produce thousands of hits, most about someone else.
- Language: Names transliterated between scripts are easy to miss without tools that handle multiple languages.
- Credibility: Disinformation and low-quality sites mean every source needs judging for accuracy, editorial oversight and independence.

Wolfsberg also asks firms to decide which offence types they screen for and at what stage, from allegation through to conviction. Screening on early-stage allegations catches more but produces more alerts and less certainty.

## A risk-based approach, not screen-everything

Banks do not have to run the same search on every customer. The Wolfsberg FAQs say screening depth, sources and frequency should follow customer risk: a high-profile client might get broad searches, a mass-market retail customer a targeted one, and some cases may need none at all. What firms do need is a documented approach that matches the rules where they operate.

The guidance is also specific about controls. Every alert should have a recorded outcome and rationale. Quality control should happen before alert decisions and quality assurance after them, ideally through statistical sampling. Management should see figures on screening volumes, false positives and true matches.

## What has happened since

Our February 2025 article pointed to the new EU AML Authority as a source of regulatory pressure. Since then it has started work. According to [AMLA's FAQs](https://www.amla.europa.eu/faqs_en), the authority was legally established on 26 June 2024, is based in Frankfurt and began operations in summer 2025. It will select about 40 obliged entities for direct supervision during 2027 and [start supervising them in 2028](https://www.amla.europa.eu/amla-advances-preparations-2027-selection-exercise_en).

The single EU rulebook, [Regulation (EU) 2024/1624](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1624/oj), applies from 10 July 2027. From then on, customer due diligence rules in EU member states come from one directly applicable regulation instead of national laws transposing a directive.

The Wolfsberg Negative News Screening FAQs published in 2022 are still the version listed on the Wolfsberg resources page in October 2026. Among the providers named above, KYC360 was acquired by Experian in October 2025.
