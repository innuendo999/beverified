Until now Ofcom's age assurance rules have been about one line: 18. The UK's planned social media ban moves the line to 16, and on 29 October 2026 Ofcom said which checks it thinks can hold it. The Secretary of State asked for the work as a rapid assessment to inform Parliament before the Government lays its detailed rules, [according to DIGIT](https://www.digit.fyi/ofcom-suggests-age-check-methods-for-under-16s/). Ofcom chief executive Dame Melanie Dawes [told ITV](https://www.itv.com/news/2026-10-29/how-could-social-media-platforms-enforce-a-ban-on-under-16s): "We should expect that motivated children will seek to bypass the restrictions."

We could not open Ofcom's own assessment page or PDF from our tools when writing this, so the details below come from law firm and press summaries of it, plus Ofcom and GOV.UK pages we did read. Each claim says where it comes from.

## The ban the checks are for

The Government [announced the ban on 15 June 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/social-media-to-be-banned-for-under-16s-in-landmark-government-move-to-givekids-their-childhood-back). It covers user-to-user platforms that let people interact, post material and use algorithms; the announcement names Snapchat, TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook and X. Messaging services such as WhatsApp and Signal are not meant to be included. Separate restrictions would stop livestreaming and contact from strangers for under-16s on a wider set of services, including gaming sites, and those restrictions would be on by default for 16 and 17 year olds.

The same announcement said Ofcom would run a rapid study of which methods can show someone is over 16, and that the protections are expected to come into force in spring 2027 through secondary legislation under the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act. [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/uk-online-safety-ofcom-assesses-highly-effective-age-assurance-methods-online-services) says the Government intends to lay the first regulations by the end of 2026, after which Ofcom will consult on draft guidance for age assurance at 16. Which services end up in scope is still unclear until the regulations are published.

## Four methods that can work at 16

[Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/uk-online-safety-ofcom-assesses-highly-effective-age-assurance-methods-online-services) and [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102o6wl/proof-of-youth-in-the-uk/) both list the same four methods Ofcom treats as capable of being highly effective at 16:

| Method | How it works | Risk Ofcom flags |
|---|---|---|
| Photo ID matching | Compares a passport or driving licence with a live image of the user | Borrowed, stolen or forged ID |
| Digital identity services | A reusable ID, usually set up once against photo ID, that can share only an age result | A child using someone else's identity |
| Banking-based checks | Age data a bank already holds, shared with the user's consent | Banks have only early-stage products |
| Facial age estimation | Estimates age from facial features, no document needed | Error near 16, spoofing with photos or video |

The risk column follows [ITV's summary](https://www.itv.com/news/2026-10-29/how-could-social-media-platforms-enforce-a-ban-on-under-16s), which also reports Ofcom calling banking checks "robust against circumvention attempts". Osborne Clarke stresses that Ofcom calls the list non-exhaustive.

Coverage is a practical problem for document routes. [DIGIT reports](https://www.digit.fyi/ofcom-suggests-age-check-methods-for-under-16s/) that about 94% of UK residents aged 16 and over hold a passport or driving licence, that around 150,000 16 and 17 year olds hold neither, and that around 90% of 16 and 17 year olds have a bank account. A service that offers only photo ID matching will turn away part of the age group it is legally allowed to serve.

## What drops out below 18

Ofcom's [age assurance guidance](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-assurance) lists seven methods capable of being highly effective at 18: credit card checks, digital identity services, email-based age estimation, facial age estimation, mobile network operator checks, open banking and photo ID matching. At 16, three of them fall away. Credit card checks, mobile network operator checks and email-based estimation can support checks at 18 but "cannot currently reliably distinguish 16- and 17-year-olds from younger children", in Osborne Clarke's summary of the assessment.

Also ruled out as a way in: self-declared birthdays, contractual age terms, verifiable parental consent in its current form, and behavioural age inference as the first gate. That last point is not new. In its [July report on age assurance](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-checks-helping-make-online-experiences-safer-for-uk-children-but-job-not-done-and-tech-industry-must-act-to-strengthen-protections) Ofcom said current inference methods are "insufficient in preventing children from accessing a service at the point of entry". We covered the investigation into TikTok's checks that followed in our [TikTok age checks story](/news/tiktoks-ai-age-checks/).

## The challenge age for facial estimation

[Facial age estimation](/glossary/facial-age-estimation/) gives a number with an error band, and at 16 that band is the problem. ITV and DIGIT both quote Ofcom saying some technologies have "a margin of error of a few years at 16". Ofcom's answer is a challenge age, the same idea as Challenge 25 at a shop till. [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/uk-online-safety-ofcom-assesses-highly-effective-age-assurance-methods-online-services) reports that Ofcom suggests a challenge age of 19 for a 16 threshold, and that providers should set it from the margin of error of the technology they actually use. Under a challenge age of 19, a user estimated at 16 to 19 would likely need a second check, [ITV notes](https://www.itv.com/news/2026-10-29/how-could-social-media-platforms-enforce-a-ban-on-under-16s).

The buffer costs something: every real 16, 17 or 18 year old who estimates under 19 goes to a second method, and the second method is usually photo ID or a bank check. Ofcom also suggests retry limits on facial estimation, testing against simple tricks to look older, and liveness detection on selfie and document checks, according to Osborne Clarke and ITV.

## App stores mostly ask

App stores sit outside the Online Safety Act today. [Advanced Television](https://www.advanced-television.com/?p=294895) reports Ofcom's finding that three quarters of children who go online say apps downloaded from an app store are their main way online, while all the app stores Ofcom analysed rely on children declaring their own age, with a small number adding checks in limited cases. Ofcom says app stores could make more use of highly effective age assurance. Its [July report](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/use-of-age-assurance-report-2026) says a statutory report on app store protections is due by January 2027, and that any change to the law is for Government and Parliament. Until then, a platform cannot lean on the store's age signal: the regulated service stays responsible for its own checks, as [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102o6wl/proof-of-youth-in-the-uk/) puts it.

## Enforcement so far

The 16 rules will land on a regulator that is already fining over age checks at 18. Ofcom's [July report](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-checks-helping-make-online-experiences-safer-for-uk-children-but-job-not-done-and-tech-industry-must-act-to-strengthen-protections) counted 23 investigations into providers of 88 adult services and seven providers fined, covering 24 sites. The largest fine was the £1.35 million issued to 8579 LLC on [23 February 2026](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/ofcom-fines-porn-company-1.35-million-for-not-having-age-checks), plus £50,000 for ignoring an information request and daily penalties until it complied. The ceiling under the Act is £18 million or 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is greater, according to Ofcom's [guidance page](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-assurance). Ofcom says it will be ready to act against companies that do not comply once the ban is in force, [DIGIT reports](https://www.digit.fyi/ofcom-suggests-age-check-methods-for-under-16s/).

## Choosing an age verification provider for UK users

If you run a service that may fall under the ban, the assessment turns into a short list of questions for vendors. None of it is legal advice; the final guidance will come after consultation.

- Which of the four methods do you offer at 16? A vendor strong on credit card or email checks may not have a 16 route at all. Ask for each method separately.
- What is your facial estimation error at 16 and 17, and what challenge age do you recommend? Ofcom wants the buffer set from the tool's own error, so you need the vendor's figure, ideally from an independent test, not a general accuracy claim.
- What happens to users inside the buffer? Check that the fallback is in the same flow and that it covers teenagers without a passport or licence, for example through a bank or digital ID route.
- What do you send back? Osborne Clarke notes Ofcom's view that a third-party provider can confirm only whether a user meets the age threshold, without passing on identifying data. Ask whether the result is a yes or no, and how long images and documents are kept.
- How do you stop spoofing? Ask about [liveness detection](/providers/liveness-detection/) on selfie and document steps, document authenticity checks and retry limits.

## What happens next

The Government's first regulations are expected by the end of 2026. Ofcom will then consult on its draft guidance for age assurance at 16, and the protections are due in spring 2027. [Advanced Television](https://www.advanced-television.com/?p=294895) reports that Ofcom will publish research on how children get around the rules within 12 months of the ban taking effect.
