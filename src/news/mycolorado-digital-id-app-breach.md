Colorado's state app, myColorado, holds the Colorado Digital ID and lets residents keep copies of other documents next to it. On 18 November 2026 someone got into that second part. The state has been posting updates on [mycolorado.gov](https://mycolorado.gov/), and the app has been offline since. Some of the figures are still estimates, and the stolen files are the kind that identity checks rely on.

## What happened

According to the state's [outage update](https://mycolorado.gov/), the app "began experiencing unusual usage patterns" late on Wednesday night, 18 November 2026. Those patterns set off automated threat detection in the state's AWS environment. The Governor's Office of Information Technology (OIT) found what appeared to be unauthorised activity and decided to take the app down altogether as a precaution. The page does not give the time the app went offline.

The state says it has "no reason to believe" the incident is part of a wider attack. It does not name an outside forensic firm or say whether law enforcement is involved.

## What data was involved

The update dated 25 November 2026 says the incident "primarily involved" the "myCards and Documents" part of the app. That is data users uploaded themselves: PDFs and photos of documents "like passports, vehicle registrations, and insurance cards." The word "like" means the list may not be complete. The state says payment information was not involved.

The state's estimate is that "roughly 100k users' data was involved," out of about 2.1 million users. It says the number will be refined as staff match each document to its owner. Before that update, [Government Technology](https://www.govtech.com/security/colorado-takes-mycolorado-app-offline-after-cyber-incident) reported a range of 50,000 to 150,000 people, and quoted Colorado CIO Sarah Tuneberg: "Protecting our users' data is our first priority, and we will bring systems back online as quickly as we can once security is restored." [Denver7](https://www.denver7.com/news/local-news/mycolorado-breach-affected-passport-registration-and-insurance-data-state-says) reported the 25 November figures with a statement from Tuneberg as state chief information officer and OIT executive director.

## What residents are told to do

The state expects notices to go out from Friday, 27 November 2026, and says each one will name the specific documents involved. The page does not mention credit monitoring. Its advice is to freeze or lock credit with the bureaus and to keep records in case reports to a bank, the police or the FTC are needed.

For identity checks while the app is down, the page says to use a physical driver licence or state ID card, and reminds residents that Colorado law requires them to carry physical ID. People who planned to fly with their mobile driver licence can add it to another DMV-approved digital wallet. The DMV's [ID on your phone page](https://dmv.colorado.gov/id-on-your-phone) lists Apple Wallet, Google Wallet and Samsung Wallet. For travellers with no acceptable ID at all, the state points to TSA ConfirmID, which costs $45. There is no confirmed date for the app to come back; the state says it will return with security enhancements.

## Why it matters for identity checks

The Colorado Digital ID lives inside myColorado, so while the app is offline it cannot be presented in the usual way (the state's FAQ notes that some phones keep part of the data cached). In January 2026 the DMV announced a [Colorado Digital ID Verifier](https://sbg.colorado.gov/dmv-alert-announcing-the-colorado-digital-idtm-verifier-in-mycolorado-app) that lets a checker scan a QR code to confirm the credential is genuine. It was released after a case in which a man was accused of spoofing the app to forge digital licences. The DMV notice says it "has authorized the acceptance" of the Digital ID, while acceptance at its own offices is optional. Businesses that took the Digital ID in person now need a fallback, and the state's own answer is the physical card.

The data taken is a different problem. A clear photo of a real passport or licence is what someone needs to try a remote [document check](/glossary/document-verification/) in another person's name. The document will pass checks for format and security features because it is real. What should stop it is the step that ties the document to the person in front of the camera: a selfie match with [liveness detection](/providers/liveness-detection/). Teams that accept uploaded images without that step, or that let a user upload a photo of a document instead of capturing it live, carry more of this risk. Our guide to [synthetic identity and deepfake fraud](/guide/synthetic-identity-and-deepfake-fraud/) covers the related attacks.

The incident also shows the trade-off in [reusable digital identity](/glossary/reusable-digital-identity/): one app that holds many documents is convenient, and it is one place to breach. Here the state says the incident mainly involved documents users chose to upload; it has not said the Digital ID credential itself was affected. When an identity vendor holds the same kind of file, the exposure looks similar, as in the [Veriff and Total Wireless breach](/news/veriff-total-wireless-data-breach/). Our [incident tracker](/incidents/) follows breaches at KYC and AML vendors; myColorado is a state service, not a vendor, so it is not listed there.

## What happens next

Notices to affected users are due to start on 27 November 2026, and the state says it will refine the 100,000 estimate as matching continues. The app stays offline until OIT is satisfied with its security changes. We will update this story when the state publishes a final count, a cause or a return date.
