Our October 2025 story ran with a round number: AML and KYC spending heading for $3 billion. The figure came from [Burton-Taylor](https://tpicap.com/burtontaylor/Research%20Headline), the market research brand of TP ICAP, which projected that global spending on AML and KYC data and services would total a record $2.9 billion in 2025, up 12.3% on the year before. Burton-Taylor also said these budgets had grown at a compound rate of 22% over the previous five years.

That is worth reading carefully. The number is what financial institutions pay data vendors for screening lists, adverse media, corporate records and related services. It is not the full cost of a bank's compliance function, which also covers staff, in-house systems and case management.

## Where the money goes

Burton-Taylor said the Americas account for about half of all AML and KYC data spending. Its [2024 report](https://burton-taylor.com/sites/g/files/escbpb181/files/burton-taylor/reports/2024-08/2024%20AML%20KYC%20Report.pdf), the latest edition with a public breakdown, estimated 2024 spend at $2.54 billion and split it like this:

| Region | 2024 estimate |
|---|---|
| Americas | $1,323.3 million |
| EMEA | $882.7 million |
| Asia | $334.8 million |

The same report shows how quickly the market has grown. Spend was $1.34 billion in 2021, so the $2.9 billion projected for 2025 is more than double it.

## The vendors in front

Four data companies hold most of the market. In the 2024 report, LexisNexis Risk Solutions had 35.3% ($898 million), LSEG 28.0% ($712 million), Moody's 12.3% and Dow Jones Risk & Compliance 11.8%. Everyone else shared the remaining 12.5%. Vendor shares for 2025 and 2026 are in the paid reports, so we have not repeated estimates we could not check.

Many platforms that banks deal with directly sit on top of these data sets. [KYC360](/providers/kyc360/), for example, uses Dow Jones data for its sanctions, PEP and adverse-media screening. Others, such as [ComplyAdvantage](/providers/complyadvantage/), build their own financial-crime database and sell screening as a platform. Our [AML software ranking](/providers/aml/) compares both types.

## Why AI pushes costs up first

Burton-Taylor tied the 2025 growth to AI-powered tools that find new correlations in customer and transaction data. The catch for banks is timing. Machine-learning screening and risk scoring need clean data, integration with older core systems and model governance before they save any analyst hours. The bill arrives before the efficiency does.

Reputation is part of the calculation. Hadley Weinberger, the Burton-Taylor senior analyst who wrote the research, put it this way: "reputations built over decades can be devastated by a single compliance incident that goes viral."

## Enforcement keeps budgets high

The penalties are large enough to justify the spend. In October 2024 TD Bank pleaded guilty to Bank Secrecy Act and money laundering conspiracy charges and agreed to pay [over $1.8 billion](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/gallery/td-bank-pleads-guilty-bank-secrecy-act-and-money-laundering-conspiracy-violations-18b) to resolve the Justice Department case. On the same day [FinCEN assessed a record $1.3 billion penalty](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-assesses-record-13-billion-penalty-against-td-bank), so the two resolutions together came to more than $3 billion. (Our original story gave a single $3.2 billion figure; we have replaced it with the amounts the two agencies published.)

## Perpetual KYC changes the spending pattern

A lot of the new spend goes on continuous monitoring. Instead of re-checking customers on a fixed review cycle, banks refresh risk when the underlying data changes: a new sanctions designation, a change of ownership, an adverse news hit. That needs live data feeds and more integration work at the start, with less manual review later. ## What has happened since

Burton-Taylor published the [2026 edition](https://burton-taylor.com/reports/07/2026/anti-money-launderingknow-your-customer-data-services-global-market-share-2026) of its AML and KYC market share report on 7 July 2026. It projects data vendors' AML and KYC revenues to reach a record $3.4 billion in 2026, up 18.2%. That means the $3 billion mark from our headline has now been passed. The report names technology, rising regulatory scrutiny and more severe penalties as the main growth drivers.

The public summary of the 2026 report does not give regional splits or vendor shares, so the tables above still use the 2024 figures, the latest ones published openly.
