FinCEN has turned a Russia-focused money laundering power on one of the world's bigger offshore crypto exchanges. A [Treasury announcement](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0685) on 6 November 2026 said US financial institutions would be barred from moving funds to or from MEXC Global, and the [order](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2026-11/MEXC-Section-9714.pdf) followed in the Federal Register four days later. For compliance teams at US banks, money services businesses and crypto firms, the practical question is whether they can spot MEXC on either side of a transfer before the order bites.

## What the order says

The operative sentence is short: "A covered financial institution is prohibited from engaging in any transmittal of funds from or to MEXC Global." A transmittal of funds means sending or receiving funds, and the order says that includes convertible virtual currency. A covered financial institution has the same meaning as "financial institution" in 31 CFR 1010.100(t), so the ban reaches banks, broker-dealers, money services businesses and the other institution types listed there.

The [Federal Register notice](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-22962.pdf) (FR Doc. 2026-22962) was published on 10 November 2026. The order takes effect 30 days after publication, which is 10 December 2026, and has no cessation date. FinCEN keeps the power to set conditions on some transmittals and to grant exemptions.

FinCEN acted under section 9714(a) of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act, as amended by section 6106(b) of the National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal year 2022. That section lets FinCEN act against a foreign institution it finds to be of primary money laundering concern in connection with Russian illicit finance. Treasury says the action is part of an initiative it calls Operation Economic Outcast.

## Who MEXC Global is

According to the order, MEXC Global is a crypto exchanger founded in 2018, incorporated in the Seychelles and operating in more than 170 countries "without a virtual asset service provider license." Treasury's release describes it as one of the 20 largest crypto exchangers in the world, with more than $1 billion in daily transactions and about $5 billion in reserves as of September 2026.

The order names the entities it covers:

| Entity | Jurisdiction |
|---|---|
| MEXC Global Ltd | Seychelles |
| MX Global Ltd | Seychelles |
| MEXC Estonia OÜ | Estonia |
| GIDA Technology AG | Switzerland |
| MXC Global Ltd | United Kingdom |
| MEXC Global Foundation Ltd (former) | Singapore |
| MEXC Singapore Pte Ltd (former) | Singapore |

The Estonian and UK entities matter for firms outside the US. The order binds only US institutions, but a European or British firm that banks in the US, or that runs its own risk scoring on exchange counterparties, now has a FinCEN finding to factor in.

## What FinCEN found

FinCEN assesses that since May 2021 MEXC Global has facilitated at least $16.7 billion in likely illicit activity, and finds it to be of primary money laundering concern in connection with Russian illicit finance. The order breaks the exposure down. Unless a different window is given, the figures cover May 2021 to May 2026.

| Exposure | Amount in the order |
|---|---|
| Russian sanctions evasion, direct and indirect | Nearly $1.5 billion |
| Direct transactions with US, EU or UK sanctioned entities | $232 million |
| Garantex, after its OFAC designation | About $129 million, continuing as recently as June 2026 |
| A7 network | At least $26 million |
| A7A5 ruble-backed stablecoin | Nearly $200 million |
| At least four UK-sanctioned exchangers, direct (May 2018 to July 2026) | Over $13 billion |
| Russian and other exchangers that allegedly do not collect customer identification, direct | About $2.9 billion |
| More than 40 identified Iranian crypto exchangers, direct | Over $1 billion |
| Central Bank of Iran, IRGC and its Qods Force | About $22 million |
| Suspected ransomware payments (January 2022 to July 2024) | $37.9 million |
| Darknet markets (September 2020 to July 2026) | $8.5 million |

Treasury's announcement adds no new OFAC designations. The counterparties it points to, such as Garantex, the A7 network and the Central Bank of Iran, were already designated.

## What covered institutions have to do

The order adds no new program requirement. FinCEN says compliance "requires no tools or competencies other than those already employed" in existing AML programs. In practice that means the [sanctions screening](/glossary/sanctions-screening/) and [transaction monitoring](/glossary/transaction-monitoring/) a firm already runs need to catch MEXC Global and its listed entities as a counterparty, whether the transfer is a wire or an on-chain payment.

Incoming crypto is the hard part, because a firm cannot always refuse it. FinCEN gives institutions room to act on the facts of a particular transaction, including when the originating address is no longer reachable. The order gives two examples: crypto that sat in an [unhosted wallet](/glossary/self-hosted-wallet/) for a long time before it arrived, and cases where a firm's own risk procedures would rule out sending funds back to MEXC Global.

Nothing else is switched off. The order does not change Bank Secrecy Act obligations, including filing a [suspicious activity report](/glossary/suspicious-activity-report/), or anyone's OFAC obligations. Willful violations can bring civil or criminal penalties under 31 U.S.C. 5321 and 5322.

## Questions to ask your screening and analytics vendors

If your firm relies on a vendor to label counterparties and wallet addresses, these are fair questions before 10 December:

- Does the product attribute deposit and hot wallet addresses to MEXC Global, and how fast are new addresses added?
- Can it match all of the entity names in the order, including the former Singapore entities, in counterparty and correspondent screening?
- Can it block an outgoing transfer before it is sent, rather than flag it afterwards?
- How does it record the decision on an incoming transfer that could not be returned, so the file shows why the firm acted as it did?
