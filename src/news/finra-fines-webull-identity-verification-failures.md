Webull grew fast after 2020, and FINRA says its controls did not. In a [news release](https://www.finra.org/media-center/newsreleases/2026/finra-fines-webull-42-million-anti-money-laundering-and-best) on 5 November 2026 the regulator said it had fined Webull Financial LLC $4.2 million for failing to keep reasonably designed programs for anti-money laundering, identity theft prevention and best execution. Bill St. Louis, FINRA's head of enforcement, said the firm's compliance program "did not keep pace" with its growth. Webull consented to the findings without admitting or denying them.

Most fines in this area are about monitoring. This one is mostly about onboarding, and about what happens when a firm sends identity checks to a vendor and then approves whatever comes back.

## Millions of accounts, one vendor, thin procedures

According to the [settlement](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/Webull-Financial-LLC-AWC-110426.pdf) (AWC No. 2021073404901), Webull approved at least 7 million new customer accounts between January 2020 and September 2023, at least 6.5 million of them using a third-party identity verification vendor. Until November 2022 it had no reasonable written procedures for verifying customer identities, no detailed procedures for manual review, and no procedures on when to file a suspicious activity report for potential identity theft. FINRA found that the program it adopted in November 2022 still was not reasonably designed.

The result, in FINRA's words, was more than 120,000 accounts approved "without forming a reasonable belief" that the firm knew its customers' true identity. The findings cite FINRA Rules [3310(b)](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/3310) and 2010 and the broker-dealer CIP rule at 31 C.F.R. 1023.220.

## What got through

The settlement lists several ways accounts were opened that should not have been:

- **Inconclusive results approved.** Webull approved some accounts where the vendor could not verify the customer's information because of inconsistencies or possible identity fraud indicators. Senior customers were approved without extra verification even when the vendor flagged logical inconsistencies or fraud risk.
- **Landscape photos as ID.** More than 50 customers submitted photos of scenic landscapes instead of government-issued identification, and their accounts were approved automatically. FINRA says Webull relied on a vendor for photo ID checks that lacked reasonable verification processes, and that Webull did not reasonably supervise that vendor. After the landscape problem was fixed, the firm and the vendor still did not catch fraudulent ID submissions.
- **Deceased applicants.** Some manual reviews approved applications without looking into identities that could not be verified, including applications where the firm's own systems indicated the customer was deceased.
- **Overloaded reviewers.** The people doing manual review handled an unreasonably high monthly volume and had no reasonable documented guidance.

FINRA does not name the vendor.

## Identity theft red flags that matched on device and IP

The identity theft findings cover January 2020 to February 2023 and cite Rule 201 of Regulation S-ID. FINRA says Webull's identity theft prevention program relied on the deficient CIP and missed red flags. In one case the firm opened roughly 80 accounts for foreign nationals who used two identical device IDs, and each account transferred in and then withdrew roughly $1,000 on the same day. In another it approved 91 accounts for foreign nationals from the same IP address, each listing an employer that could not be verified and a temporary email domain. Webull did not check for multiple unrelated accounts using the same Social Security number until August 2021.

## Monitoring and best execution

The AML surveillance findings follow a familiar pattern. Between January 2020 and September 2023 Webull did not identify or investigate 473 unauthorised ACH transfers, totalling more than $1.5 million, moved from customers' accounts at other institutions. One account generated nearly 13,000 alerts for potentially manipulative trading in more than 100 stock symbols over about 18 months and was not investigated in time. The firm had no surveillance for unrelated customers trading from the same address or device, and could not detect account takeovers in time.

The best execution findings, from at least February 2020 to at least October 2025, concern how Webull reviewed execution quality against competing market centres. They are outside the scope of identity verification, so we leave them there. ## What buyers of identity verification should test

None of this is a verdict on identity verification software as such. FINRA's point is that Webull did not set rules for what the vendor's answers meant and did not check the vendor's work. A buyer can test for each of these gaps before signing or during a proof of concept:

- **What happens to "could not verify".** List every result status the vendor returns and decide in writing which ones may auto-approve. An inconclusive or fraud-flagged result that flows into an approval by default is the first failure in this case.
- **Junk-image tests.** Upload a landscape, a screenshot, a printed copy and a photo of a screen during the trial. A [document verification](/glossary/document-verification/) step should reject anything that is not an identity document, and you should see why it was rejected.
- **Signals the vendor returns that your rules ignore.** Deceased indicators, SSN mismatches and data inconsistencies only help if your decision logic acts on them. Ask the vendor for the full list of fields and check each one is mapped.
- **Links across applications.** Duplicate SSNs, shared device IDs and shared IP addresses are cross-customer checks. Some tools run them, others leave them to you; ask which, and test with seeded duplicates. Our guide on [synthetic identity and deepfake fraud](/guide/synthetic-identity-and-deepfake-fraud/) covers the patterns.
- **Manual review capacity.** Size the review queue against your expected volume, and write the guidance reviewers follow. FINRA treated an overloaded queue without guidance as a control failure in its own right.
- **Vendor oversight.** Keep evidence that you test the vendor's accuracy over time, such as sampled re-reviews and error rates. FINRA faulted Webull for not supervising its vendor.

## What happens next

Under the settlement, a member of Webull's senior management must certify in writing, within 180 days of FINRA accepting it, that the firm has remediated its identity theft prevention and AML issues. Webull must also retain an independent consultant within 60 days to review its best execution program, adopt the consultant's recommendations or propose alternatives, and report on implementation. The fine is payable on acceptance. FINRA's release notes a separate 2025 action, in which it censured Webull and fined it $1.6 million over the supervision of influencer social media promotions.
